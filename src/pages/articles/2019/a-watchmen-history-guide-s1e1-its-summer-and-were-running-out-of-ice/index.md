HBO’s **_Watchmen_** takes place in an alternate 2019 when America won the Vietnam War and Robert Redford has been president for the past 30 years. The officers of the Tulsa Police Department are the new masked anti-heroes of the **_Watchmen_** universe, wearing yellow masks and adopting entire secret personas to fight a white supremacist group named the Seventh Kavalry. 

The first episode, "It’s Summer and We’re Running Out of Ice_,"_ is dense with historic details like Nixon and Bass Reeves. It was clear to us at Lewton Bus that, after watching the episode a few times, there was a lot to talk about and a lot to learn. 

So every week, **_Lewton Bus_** will publish a concise (and admittedly incomplete) history guide to the quintessentially _American_ references in **_Watchmen_**. Every week, we’ll post links, brief discussions, and explanations related to each episode and we hope you’ll follow along and discuss with us. 

 

## **Bass Reeves** 

The silent film in the beginning of the episode depicts a tall man in a dark hood hauling a corrupt and thieving sheriff to face the townspeople. The man throws back his hood to reveal a striped uniform. The next bit of dialogue, displayed on a card, is “it’s Bass Reeves! The Black Marshal of Oklahoma!” 

You might know by now, but Bass Reeves was a real person in Oklahoma. Born in 1838 to parents who were enslaved, Reeves became “the first black U.S. Deputy Marshal west of the Mississippi River” ([Twitter](https://twitter.com/bhamcivilrights/status/960512228322627584)). The silent film being shown, called [_Trust in the Law!_](https://www.hbo.com/content/dam/hbodata/series/watchmen/peteypedia/01/trust-in-the-law.pdf), is not a real film but is made to look like one, down to attributing it to Oscar Micheaux, one of the first Black filmmakers. 

More on Bass Reeves: 

- The song, “He’s a Lone Ranger” on Don Flemons’s 2018 album, [_Black Cowboys_](https://www.npr.org/2019/01/05/682318409/dom-flemons-presents-a-new-image-of-the-american-cowboy). Flemons, who is of African-American and Mexican descent, created _Black Cowboys_ as a collection of songs about African-Americans who were instrumental to American history and the West.  
- If you’re really into it, maybe plan a trip to the [Bass Reeves Western History Conference](https://www.bassreevesconference.com) in Muskogee, Oklahoma. 

 

## **Tulsa Massacre** 

The silent film is interrupted by a foreboding siren and mass panic, as a little boy turns and is immediately swept up by his father. He says, “it’s time to go” and the two, along with the boy’s mother, emerge from the theater into the streets of Tulsa. Chaos has erupted, with bodies everywhere, planes attacking from the sky, and white men shooting black residents. 

The Tulsa massacre, which also took place in 1921, is a piece of horrific history that many people have never heard of and was certainly never covered in my history classes. White residents, including the KKK, burned down Greenwood, a neighborhood known as “Black Wall Street.” It had been home to an affluent Black community. The white perpetrators burned down businesses and homes and killed hundreds, and many of the victims were allegedly buried in mass graves. 

Perhaps most damning is how the city government was complicit in and responsible for the violence. According to a curator at the [National Museum of African American History and Culture](http://nmaahc.si.edu/), “It was the frustration of poor whites not knowing what to do with a successful black community, and in coalition with the city government were given permission to do what they did.” 

More on the Tulsa Massacre (and not really enough): 

- An [interactive timeline](https://www.tulsahistory.org/exhibit/1921-tulsa-race-massacre/) at The Tulsa Historical Society and Museum
- [Dr. Olivia J. Hooker](https://www.npr.org/sections/codeswitch/2018/05/31/615546965/meet-the-last-surviving-witness-to-the-tulsa-race-riot-of-1921), one of the last surviving witnesses, speaks about her experiences (NPR) and [more video with Dr. Hooker](https://twitter.com/phil_lewis_/status/1186269925226270720?s=21). 
- [**_The Burning_**](https://www.indiebound.org/book/9780312302474) by Tim Madigan - this was one of the books that Nicole Kassell, who directed the first episode, and Damon Lindelof, who created the show, drew from and referenced. 
- The Smithsonian published an [eyewitness account](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/long-lost-manuscript-contains-searing-eyewitness-account-tulsa-race-massacre-1921-180959251/) that described planes circling over town.
- The [New York Times](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/10/21/arts/television/watchmen-tulsa-race-riot.html) has a very long and fantastic list of resources. 

 

## **_Oklahoma!_** 

Rogers and Hammerstein’s iconic musical is “[about whiteness](https://theundefeated.com/features/the-unbearable-whiteness-of-oklahoma-on-broadway/)” writes Soraya Nadia McDonald. “**_Oklahoma!_** delivers a rose-tinted view of history that centers on happy white people whose greatest concern is a town dance that will raise money to build a new school. It’s a classic example of willful erasure and ahistorical mythmaking.” 

That’s why a production with a Black cast member in a lead role, like the one McDonald writes about, is so notable (well, aside from the fact that it’s also being hailed as Sexy **_Oklahoma!_**). It’s also why a production depicted in **_Watchmen_**, with a completely Black cast, is important. It’s not just a musical “about whiteness” eschewing that whiteness for Black faces, it’s the use of **_Oklahoma!_** that hammers home that this show is quintessentially _American_, full of hope for the West but also racism and erasing POC from the narrative. 

- Have you ever heard about the great land rush that created Oklahoma City? It’s bizarre. This [99% Invisible episode](https://99percentinvisible.org/episode/the-worst-way-to-start-a-city/) takes you into the history of how white settlers “claimed” almost 2 million square miles by running into it and literally just building wherever they put their tools. 

 

## **Vietnam** 

Angela says she was born in Vietnam a few years before it became a state. In this alternate 2019, it’s inferred that because America won the Vietnam War, it then annexed Vietnam and made it a state. In our timeline, millions of people died in a conflict whose origins are largely still puzzling and complicated to most Americans. 

More on the Vietnam War: 

- Ken Burns did an 18-hour documentary on it and [here’s the first episode on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G9pIuCu-YpM)

 

## **“Redfordations” / Reparations** 

Angela, whose cover story is that she owns a bakery, is speaking at her son’s school to teach the kids about banh bia, or mooncakes (they’re delicious, by the way). Afterward, a smarmy kid asks, “did Redfordations pay for it?” 

“Redfordations” is a play on “Robert Redford,” who is president, and “reparations” which is a word with _a lot of meaning._ In fact, [it’s not clear that Americans even agree on what reparations are](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2019/06/reparations-definition-2020-candidates/590863/), only that they imply that the United States government acknowledges the accurate and full history of the United States, which includes its systematic oppression of Black people. 

In the context of this boy’s question, there is clearly a monetary element to it - that is, money was eventually given to those who had been wronged (i.e., people who descended from enslaved families or who faced discrimination as a result of Jim Crow and other racist laws and policies). It’s also clear from his tone and from Topher’s reaction, to punch his classmate, that he doesn’t hold reparations in high regard. 

More on Reparations: 

- Ta-Nehisi Coates’s incredible article, [The Case for Reparations](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2014/06/the-case-for-reparations/361631/), is basically the first thing you need to read. 
- The second thing is probably Coates [discussing that landmark article](https://www.newyorker.com/news/the-new-yorker-interview/ta-nehisi-coates-revisits-the-case-for-reparations) 5 years after it was published. 
- To understand more about the legacy of slavery and Jim Crow, read articles from The New York Times’s [1619 Project](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2019/08/14/magazine/1619-america-slavery.html) and **[_The Warmth of Other Suns_](https://www.indiebound.org/book/9780679763888)** by Isabel Wilkerson, about the mass migration of Black Americans to all corners of the country (it also happens to be one of my favorite books). 

 

## **Robert Redford** 

I was surprised that this came up when the _**Lewton Bus**_ crew discussed the episode. Just because you’re in a Marvel movie doesn’t mean you’ve got name recognition! In our timeline, Robert Redford [retired](https://www.vanityfair.com/hollywood/2018/09/robert-redford-the-old-man-and-the-gun-interview) in 2018 after a prolific and legendary acting career, including perhaps his most famous movie role, Bob Woodward, in **_All the President’s Men_**. Bob Woodward, if you don’t know, was one of the very real journalists (alongside Carl Bernstein) who broke open the Watergate story and ushered in the end of the Nixon presidency. In **_Watchmen_**, Redford has already been president for 30 years, and his liberal policies have angered the white supremacist movement. Note that it doesn’t appear that Redford himself will make a cameo. 

 

## **Nixonville** 

When Angela gets home from her son’s school event, she finds out that the Tulsa Police Chief has issued an alert. Going to her bakery and donning her Sister Night uniform (which is sure to be iconic now), she kicks down a door or two at Nixonville, a destitute trailer park, and kidnaps a white supremacist goon. 

As [Adam Bumas](https://lewtonbus.net/author/adam-bumas/) astutely pointed out, “I don't know how much sense 'Nixonville' makes if you don't know about Hoovervilles.” Of course, you _can_ understand that a “Nixonville” is just the name for the trailer park. But when placed next to the context of Hoovervilles, it changes your perception. 

What’s a Hooverville? During the Great Depression, a massive collapse in the United States economy which occurred from 1929 to 1939, poor residents established shantytowns in several states and cities, including New York City. These ad hoc neighborhoods were nicknamed “Hoovervilles” - a sneering tribute to Herbert Hoover, the president who many residents blamed for their plight. 

Another historical element of Nixonville is the big, cartoony statue of Nixon that’s at the center of the trailer park. We only get to see it from behind, but it’s clear that it’s a reference to several similar poses he struck, including in this [campaign photo](https://cdn.nixonlibrary.org/01/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/13111943/Nixon-Campaigns-in-Chicago1.jpg) taken in Chicago in 1968. In the photo, his arms are outstretched and his index and middle finger are shaped into Vs. His blue suit, his smile, and the confetti behind him paint a portrait of victory as he would become president just a month later. 

More on Hoovervilles: 

- Mashable has a [very good series of photos](https://mashable.com/2015/09/17/hoovervilles/) of Hoovervilles around the country 
- The U.S. Archives has a poignant video with [oral histories from the Great Depression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TpfY8kh5lUw)
- “[We’d Like to Thank You, Herbert Hoover](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cqvYNoiPDKw),” from the musical production of _Annie_, depicts the poverty of a Hooverville community
