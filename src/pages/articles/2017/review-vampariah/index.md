Matthew Abaya's _**Vampariah**_ Is difficult to evaluate with the critical lens that you’d typically turn on a mainstream film. It is clearly a labor of love, a true outsider film that does its best to deliver professional film craft but is rough enough that it seems redundant to talk about its formal qualities.  


  
I will get the tough stuff out of the way first: by conventional formal standards this is a mediocre film, at least in all of the ways that require a lot of money and people. It is under-lit, unevenly shot if frequently very skillfully composed, occasionally bafflingly edited, and its production design and effects are middling. As a committed fan of outsider, trash, and queer film, however, I enjoyed _**Vampariah**_ on its own terms as a heartfelt amateur piece, an entertaining homage to beloved genre tropes and an earnest rebuttal to the embedded racism and misogyny that dominates so much of mainstream film. Its screenplay is both goofily self-serious and comedic, and performances are directed to the height of campiness, all of which make it a lot of fun.

On the surface its story is yawningly familiar. A vampire underground wages an endless war in San Francisco with a militarized police force, well-armed martial artists who call themselves hunters. The film does subversive things with its premise, however. First of all, it heavily incorporates Filipino folklore, rewriting the canonical rules according to conventions that are new to most western films. It opens with a silly battle with a hopping vampire, familiar to many who have dipped into the well of Asian body horror. The hunters are after a Filipino strain of vampire called Aswangs (the name is a source of humor in a regrettable scene with a doofus who keeps calling them “asswangs”). Aswangs  move like hummingbirds, hovering then sliding rapidly from enemy to enemy.  
They all have bat-like wings, an old Western vampire trope that’s largely disappeared in the age of sexy teenage vampire soap opera .  


  
Presumably wings aren't cost-effective enough for television, but if a scrappy effort like this figures it out, _**Vampire Diaries**_ has no excuse. These vampires also leave their legs behind when they fly into battle. This is a wholly unique (to these eyes) and memorable idea: bisected vampires dogfighting in pre-dawn light as their legs wait for them to return. The film opens with a sort of origin story for the Aswang, and clumsily inserted exposition fills in more about them as we get closer to them in the film.

\[caption id="attachment\_2086" align="alignnone" width="639"\]![](http://lewtonbus.net/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/mahal-720x450.gif) Mahal, surrounded by calligraphic banners that act on the undead as metaphysical handcuffs\[/caption\]

Secondly, the film has no place for patriarchy, misogyny, or conventional gender roles. Of its many conceptual victories, this is the greatest in my eye. The film follows one hunter, Mahal, as she undertakes a mission to destroy an Aswang who is terrorizing San Francisco (or something - plot mechanics are indecipherable at times). As she grows closer to the case she discovers a predictable secret about herself (we’ve all seen **_Blade_**) and finds herself at war with a violent, rogue branch of her organization. She partners with an Aswang called Bampinay, and after a night out dancing at an industrial club, followed by some violence in an alley, we can see that the attraction between them is electric. Back home, Bampinay encourages her to open up about herself and her emerging vampirism to her family, that she is “such as closet case.” Almost by definition, horror stories provide metaphors via which characters can explore and resolve challenging emotional journeys; I quite like that _**Vampariah**_ explicitly links her vampirism to her sexuality, which she is simultaneously coming to understand. _**Vampariah**_ has some visual fun at the expense of misogyny as well. Another charming, unique characteristic of the Aswang: they use massive, elongated tongues to puncture and drink from their enemies. When Mahal is nearly sexually assaulted by one of the villains, the eye-rollingly named Kilmore (no joke, and the lead villain is another Aswang named Kilman), she penetrates his lower torso with her projectile weapon. This is all very on-the-nose but it's what elevates this film above many other horror-action projects.

This is the sort of film that I think may do well at midnight screenings with audiences for are hungry for relatable genre fare.  
It is a complete film, made for nothing by a team that wanted to tell an ambitious story from a very welcome multi-cultural and queer point of view, and as such I appreciate it. I call it “trash” because it really is the definition of outsider art that, in its way, is necessary.
