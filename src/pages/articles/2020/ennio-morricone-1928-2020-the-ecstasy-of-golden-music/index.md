The world, in a state of turmoil for the past several years, had one more shovelful of dirt tossed over the coffin of entertainment with the [passing of the superb Ennio Morricone](https://www.npr.org/2020/07/06/516840947/ennio-morricone-the-sound-of-the-american-west-dies-at-91) (1928-2020). Morricone, best known for his work in the Italo-Western (or [“Spaghetti”](https://lewtonbus.net/editorials/westerns-101-spaghetti-western/)) genre, was a talent who transcended the genre, making his mark on popular culture with hits that not only became staples anyone could whistle, but put that same skill to use in horror, gangster films, and the medium as a whole.

Leone first came to prominence in our pop culture with Sergio Leone’s **_Dollars Trilogy_**, featuring Clint Eastwood and Lee Van Cleef in a loose series of movies. Morricone created sparse, weird soundscapes evoking the sere deserts the films were set in. Choirs and whistling backed the hard men whose faces eclipsed the screens, and the adventures and violence they embarked on, gunning each other down as soon as look at each other. It was not only a new eye on the Western genre itself, but a new ear on the sound of Western as a whole.

 <iframe src="//www.youtube.com/embed/h1PfrmCGFnk" width="700" height="393" allowfullscreen="allowfullscreen"></iframe>

Often credited with inventing the Western soundtrack, the truth is that Morricone _re_\-invented it. Western soundtracks can be really delineated into Before and After Morricone periods. Up until he came on the scene, Westerns had luscious soundtracks full of brassy horns and rolling drums like the thunder on the big sky prairies the movies were set in. Elmer Bernstein’s score for [**_The Magnificent Seven_**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8XDB7GMnbUQ) is a perfect example of the larger than life soundscape the genre had up until the Italo-Western came onto the scene. On the other end of the spectrum, after the first lonely whistles and bell peals of Morricone’s theme for [**_A Fistful of Dollars_**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HjjDOdaFZg0), the genre would never be the same. By the time his driving, choral "Ecstasy of Gold" played over the final cemetery confrontation in **_The Good, The Bad, and the Ugly_**, his mark was indelible. There wouldn’t be a person in the world unfamiliar with his music.

<iframe src="//www.youtube.com/embed/ubVc2MQwMkg" width="700" height="393" allowfullscreen="allowfullscreen"></iframe>

Besides his other collaborations with Leone (and another Sergio, Corbucci, who made acidic bullets to the heart with films like **_The Great Silence_**), Hollywood had taken notice. While Morricone toiled away in the Italian cinema as it churned out genre piece after genre piece, his music often outstripped the movies he scored -- though not all were bad. **_Death Rides a Horse_** is another dark, wonderful movie with a [delicious Morricone soundtrack](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ff-kQ-UmflA). His total number of scores is in the realm of over 550 pieces. But even if he wasn’t doing mainstream Hollywood work just yet, Hollywood’s biggies at the time like Sam Peckinpah were having Morricone-alike scores created for films like [**_The Wild Bunch_**](https://lewtonbus.net/editorials/western-month-sam-peckinpah-a-closer-look-part-1/).

<iframe src="//www.youtube.com/embed/5EdJabFFXt0" width="700" height="393" allowfullscreen="allowfullscreen"></iframe>

Don Siegel, attempting to recreate the Spaghetti success of newly-minted megastar Clint Eastwood for wholly American audiences, recruited Morricone to score **_Two Mules For Sister Sara_** which Morricone did with gusto, using electronic sounds to create a buzzing, twangy soundtrack complete with hee-haws to echo those mules. As a minor piece of a major career, it alone has seen usage in the recent movies [**_Django Unchained_**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XAWpIQATcRE) and [**_Sherlock Holmes: A Game of Shadows_**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ah-KrvcwJkY).\[note\]Morricone would finally go on to win an “I’m sorry we ignored your entire career” Oscar for his original score on another Quentin Tarantino Western, [**_The Hateful Eight_**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PTwWp6VMNGs).\[/note\]

<iframe src="//www.youtube.com/embed/NNbLE5Eznu0" width="700" height="393" allowfullscreen="allowfullscreen"></iframe>

From there, Morricone became a Hollywood player, which led to him branching out from the Western genre. He first turned to horror with the soundtrack to [**_The Exorcist II_**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lB2NsgO0PVA), which like much of his 60s Italian work, far surpassed the movie it was made for. Where he really proved himself was with John Carpenter’s 1982 horror all-timer, **_The Thing_**. Morricone not only provided another creepy, sparse, electronic soundscape for hard men killing each other in the Antarctic wastes (rather than the Western desert), but he also proved he could play in other people’s wheelhouses, crafting a score that was as Carpenter-ish as anything Carpenter himself ever produced.

<iframe src="//www.youtube.com/embed/oa3WTj_vuvc" width="700" height="393" allowfullscreen="allowfullscreen"></iframe>

Once the 1980s really got started, Morricone also came into the gangster genre in a big way. He scored his friend Sergio Leone’s final movie, the gangster epic -- and for my money, better than **_The Godfather_** -- [**_Once Upon A Time In America_**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jj5Xczethmw). From there he went on to score Brian De Palma’s **_The Untouchables_**, finally giving the property (based on the 1960s Robert Stack TV series) a proper theme. After that came the Warren Beatty-starring [**_Bugsy_**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jd4n7N1V8WA) in 1991.

<iframe src="//www.youtube.com/embed/Hu2FekMaTQc" width="700" height="393" allowfullscreen="allowfullscreen"></iframe>

During this whole time he hadn’t given up on his home country or the industry where he’d gotten his start. Scoring over a dozen movies a year for decades, Morricone lent his practiced ear to seemingly whoever offered him a challenge and when there wasn’t one would challenge himself. In doing so he not only re-defined genres, but left his signature on others as much as any other Hollywood composer had. Morricone’s legacy is such that his music has been taken by popular culture, re-adapted for advertisements, for other shows and films, and used so often that even if someone was unfamiliar with any directly scored film he was a part of, they still know his work. And there’s one more thing any audience member who knows Morricone knows for certain: his work was always good—never bad and certainly never ugly.
